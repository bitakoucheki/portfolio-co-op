01 · Purpose

Why this exists

A resume can tell you I worked on data pipelines this summer. It cannot tell you that I spent my first two weeks deliberately not writing code, or that the most useful thing I did all term was write documentation, or that one of my three goals did not get finished. Those are the parts I actually learned from, and they have nowhere to live on a one page PDF.

So this is a learning portfolio rather than a highlight reel. Every section here pairs something I did with the reasoning behind it, including the reasoning that turned out to be wrong. That is a slightly riskier way to present yourself, and I think it is a more useful one. Anyone can list technologies. Far fewer people can tell you why they chose one approach over another and what it cost them.

Reflection is not describing what happened. It is noticing the thing you would otherwise walk straight past.

I built it for three readers. An employer who wants evidence instead of adjectives and can judge the thinking for themselves. My future self, who will have forgotten the specifics of this term within a year and will want a record detailed enough to be worth something. And me, right now, because writing an idea down is the fastest way to find out whether I actually understand it. That third reader is the reason the writing here is not always flattering.

The work term ends in August and school starts again in September, but this is built to keep taking new material for as long as it stays useful. That was the point of building it properly instead of assembling it once.

01 · Origin

Curiosity first, code second

I have always been more interested in why something behaves the way it does than in getting to the right answer quickly. Patterns, puzzles, systems that do something unexpected. That instinct is what eventually pointed me at computer science, and once I got there I realised the part I cared about was not writing code for its own sake. It was using data and AI to find patterns, support better decisions, and build things that improve as they run.

I am a Computer Science student at York University's Lassonde School of Engineering, graduating in 2027. I grew up in Iran and moved to Canada for university. Leaving meant rebuilding routines, friendships, and a sense of competence from nothing, and it taught me something I have leaned on ever since: growth almost never happens in comfortable conditions, and uncertainty is usually where the useful learning is hiding.

Choosing York was deliberate. I wanted strong technical foundations attached to real industry experience, and co-op was the mechanism. It worked. Applying coursework to problems that had actual consequences is what narrowed my interest from "software, broadly" to data engineering, analytics, and AI, which is where technical rigour and curiosity meet something that matters outside the classroom.

Outside of technical work I have always kept one foot in something creative. I studied classical piano for years, and more recently photography, abstract drawing, and visual design have become genuine interests rather than passing ones. They are not a separate life from the engineering. They are where a lot of my instincts about structure and attention came from, and I have written about that connection in .

02 · Working style

What actually motivates me

My values assessment did not surprise me, which I take as a good sign. What ranked highest was creativity, flexibility, meaningful collaboration, and continuous learning. I do not do well in rigid, repetitive structures and I am not motivated by status. What engages me is a problem worth solving, people worth solving it with, and enough room to approach it in a way that makes sense to me.

That shows up in how I work. On the analytics and data team this term I have not been content to run a query and move on. I want to know why the data looks the way it does, where the gaps are, and whether the architecture around it could be stronger. I do not have to force that curiosity. It is the same instinct that made me build a scheduling tool because I thought existing calendar apps were solving the wrong problem, and a music engine because I wanted to know what happens when a composition is written as logic instead of as a fixed timeline.

Early on I believed I had to know everything before speaking. Coursework, group projects, and this co-op have steadily corrected that. Learning happens through asking, sharing, and sometimes being visibly wrong. I still value precision, but I have become much more comfortable with uncertainty and with learning in public.

03 · Weaknesses

Where I fall short

I am not interested in dressing these up. When something interests me I take on more than is comfortable, and the calendar does not care how interested I am. That means I have had to get deliberate about scoping and prioritisation rather than assuming I will absorb the overflow. All three of my SMART goals connect back to this, and one of them was only partially met for exactly this reason. That is documented plainly in .

The second one is communication timing. My instinct when stuck is to keep working the problem alone, partly because asking feels like conceding I could not handle it. In an environment where everyone around me holds context I do not, that is a bad trade. I improved on it over the term. Improved is not the same as solved.

The third is confidence in rooms where I am the least experienced person present. That is uncomfortable and I am working on it on purpose, because being technically solid and invisible does not help anyone, least of all the team.

04 · How I learn

Hands on the system, then back to the theory

I learn by doing, then reading. Give me a real system to poke at and I will understand its behaviour far faster than I would from documentation alone, and then the theory lands because I have something to attach it to. Professionally, that means I do best where I can ask questions, experiment, and iterate, rather than being expected to arrive fully formed.

It also means I work well with people who are honest about what they do not know. Teams where the experienced members admit uncertainty are teams where a co-op student can ask a real question, and that difference determines how much anyone actually learns in four months.

Two people have shaped this most. A professor at York who treated difficulty as an opportunity rather than a warning sign, and who never adjusted what he asked of me based on how far along I was. That changed how I read hard problems: I stopped interpreting difficulty as evidence I was in the wrong place and started reading it as evidence I was in the right one. The second is a Vice President at Meridian, whose conversations gave me a view of what a technology career looks like beyond technical skill. She has been around long enough to see patterns that are not visible from where I stand, and hearing someone with that much experience name things I was only beginning to sense has been grounding in a way that is hard to overstate.

If I had to pick one artifact that represents me, it would be a photograph of my desk while I am building something new. Code on one screen, handwritten notes, a couple of books, sketches, and music playing. That mix is not a distraction from the work. It is how the work gets done.

01 · Learning goals

S.M.A.R.T. goals for the term

I wrote these to be checkable. Every milestone is a document that either exists or does not, which makes it hard to quietly move the goalposts later. The honest results are in .

Goal 01 of 03

Get better at diagnosis, not just at fixing

Critical Thinking & Problem Solving

By August 31, 2026, I will independently diagnose and resolve at least three distinct technical problems arising in my day to day data work, whether a pipeline failure, a data quality inconsistency, or an unexpected query result. For each one I will write a short post-mortem of a page or less covering what the problem was, what I tried, why I chose the resolution I did, and what I would do differently.

The point is not to catalogue every bug. It is to force myself to slow down and think analytically about what actually happened, which is the easiest thing to skip when you are in the middle of building and the fix is right there.

This matters because technical problem solving is not really about finding the fix. It is about holding a mental model of a system accurate enough to reason about it when it misbehaves. Writing the reasoning down is how I make that thinking permanent instead of letting it evaporate with the ticket.

02 · Work portfolio

Things I have built

Three projects that between them cover full stack systems, generative audio, and relational design. Each one started from a question rather than an assignment brief.

TaskPilot

AI-assisted task management system

I built this because I thought calendar apps were solving the wrong problem. They are very good at recording what you said you would do and completely indifferent to whether it is possible. TaskPilot pairs calendar style scheduling with workload balancing: it detects an overloaded schedule, suggests a better distribution, and reschedules around timing, priority, and availability rather than just flagging a conflict and leaving you to it.

Implementation

Built on the PERN stack with a normalised PostgreSQL schema, RESTful APIs in Express and Node, and a React front end supporting calendar views and prioritisation logic.

Outcome

A more adaptive alternative to a conventional calendar, with conflict detection, dynamic rescheduling, and context aware organisation as the core rather than as add-ons.

PostgreSQLExpressReact Node.jsREST APIs

Motif

Live-coded generative music engine

This one came out of a question I could not stop turning over: what if a composition had logic instead of just notes? Motif is a browser based system where music is written as conditions and loops rather than a fixed timeline, so a piece evolves, reacts, and occasionally does something I did not plan. Years of classical training and a production background are both sitting underneath it.

Implementation

A custom JavaScript DSL layered over the Web Audio API, with modular audio modules, an event scheduler, and a scrolling piano roll canvas that visualises each beat as it fires.

Outcome

Complete tracks produced end to end, from rhythm coding through to mastering, demonstrating modular audio design, real time visualisation, and the point where engineering and a classical music background stop being separate things.

JavaScriptWeb Audio API Tone.js / StrudelCanvas

Hotel Booking Database

Relational data system

A normalised relational database for a hotel booking platform, designed from ER diagrams all the way through to production style SQL. Less visually interesting than the other two and probably the one that taught me the most about the cost of getting a schema wrong early.

Implementation

Modelled rooms, guests, and reservations with enforced referential integrity, then wrote and tuned the SQL that manages those relationships and extracts insight from booking data.

Outcome

A consistent schema with performant queries that simulate real booking workflows and make whole categories of data inconsistency structurally impossible.

01 · Learning journal

Four months, in order

Written as the term went rather than reconstructed at the end. Each entry closes with what I actually took from it, which is rarely what I expected going in.

May, weeks one and two

Deliberately not writing code

The first stretch was almost entirely orientation: how the platform fits together, who owns which layer, where the boundaries between teams sit. Coming straight from coursework, my instinct was to produce something immediately. That instinct was wrong here. When data moves across business lines and governance is not optional, understanding the system is the work, and writing code before you understand it just produces confident mistakes.

So I read existing pipelines, sat in meetings where I understood maybe half the vocabulary, and wrote down every acronym I did not recognise to look up later rather than derailing the room.

What I took from it At school, speed reads as competence. In production, patience does. A question on day three is cheap. Finding the same gap on day sixty is not.
Mid May

Real work, sooner than expected

By the middle of May I was contributing directly to a project close to going into production. The stakes changed immediately. It was the first time my work sat inside something with real downstream consumers instead of a rubric and a due date.

What surprised me was how little of the difficulty was technical. The transformation logic was solvable. The hard part was working out what the data was supposed to mean to the people relying on it, which meant conversations well outside my own team.

What I took from it Correctness in a business context is not "the query runs." It is "this number matches what the person reading it believes it means."
June

Learning to ask a better question

The project pulled in business banking, wealth, and marketing, and each group held a different mental model of the same underlying data. Part of my job quietly became reconciling those models rather than moving records between systems. My questions got noticeably sharper in this stretch. Not "does this look right," but "if this field is null for a closed account, does it roll into the segment total or get excluded."

I also found out how open the organisation actually is. People in roles nowhere near mine made time to explain how they use the outputs and what they wish they could see. I took almost all of them up on it.

What I took from it A vague question gets a vague answer and costs someone twenty minutes. A specific question gets a decision. Doing the thinking before you ask is a form of respect.
Late June into July

Presenting to people well above my level

My team presents what they are working on so others can weigh in. I found this uncomfortable at first and useful almost immediately, because explaining a design out loud exposes the parts you have not fully reasoned through faster than any amount of quiet self review.

Over the term it extended past my team to directors from other groups and a Vice President. Preparing for those meant stripping out implementation detail and leading with the decision and its consequences, which is a completely different skill from explaining code to another engineer. I was not good at it the first time. I was measurably better by the third.

What I took from it Technical depth does not translate upward on its own. Knowing which detail to leave out is its own competency, and it is learnable through repetition.
July into August

The most useful thing I did all term was write

The last stretch shifted toward consolidation: documenting what I built well enough that someone else could pick it up, and closing the loops still open. Writing that documentation turned out to be the single most clarifying activity of the term. Twice I discovered mid-sentence that I could not justify a choice I had made, and went back and changed it.

It also confirmed something I already half believed from earlier work in AI evaluation: articulating why a system behaves the way it does is exactly where the flaws surface. Building and interrogating are not sequential phases. They are the same activity done properly.

What I took from it If I cannot write down why a design is the way it is, I do not understand it yet. That test has been reliable enough that I intend to keep using it on purpose.

03 · Critique

What I would do differently

Three critiques: of my own performance, of this portfolio as an artifact, and of the peer review process I took part in.

Critique of my own work term

The strongest thing I did was treat understanding as a prerequisite instead of an obstacle. The weakest thing I did was under-communicate early. When I was stuck in the first month my default was to keep working the problem alone for longer than was useful, partly because asking felt like admitting I could not do it. In an environment where everyone around me held context I did not, that is a bad trade and I made it repeatedly.

I got better at it. Better is not solved. If I ran this term again I would set an explicit rule: if I have been stuck past a set window and have learned nothing new in it, I say so out loud. Not as a request to be rescued, but as information the team can act on.

The second thing I would change is scoping. I said yes without checking what saying yes displaced. At work that caused no visible problem, because work has deadlines and deadlines force prioritisation on your behalf. Everywhere else it did, which is exactly why Goal 03 came in partial.

The pattern underneath both is the same. I am comfortable with hard problems and uncomfortable with declaring limits. That is the thing to work on next.

Critique of this portfolio

Hand building this in HTML rather than using a template platform was the right call for me, because it lets the design carry the same identity as everything else I make. It has real costs and I would rather name them than pretend otherwise. It is slower to update than a hosted editor, and every new section is a manual edit instead of a form field.

The bigger limitation is evidence. Much of what I did this term lives inside an organisation and cannot be shown. What I can offer is the reasoning around it, which is weaker proof and asks the reader to extend more trust. I have tried to compensate by being specific about process rather than vague about results, but the gap is real.

If I rebuilt it, I would design for accumulation from the beginning: one consistent entry format so that adding a project a year from now is a five minute job rather than a redesign.

On giving and receiving critique

Reviewing classmates' portfolios changed how I read my own. Seeing a range of answers to the same assignment made the effect of structural choices obvious in a way that rereading my own draft never could. Sites that led with a clear point of view were immediately more convincing than sites that led with a list of completed assessments.

Writing the feedback taught me something too. My first drafts of peer comments were longer and more hedged than they needed to be. Useful critique is short, specific, and addressed to the person rather than performed for the instructor. I cut them down deliberately.

Receiving it is the part I still find harder than I would like. My honest first reaction to criticism is to explain, and explaining is usually just defending with better manners. The more useful response is to ask what specifically produced that impression.

04 · Vision

My vision as an engineer

Where I want this to go, and what I think the field owes the people it builds for.

My vision is a future where AI systems are built to actually be trusted: not just capable, but correct, consistent, and accountable. The more these systems touch real decisions about real people, the more it matters that someone is asking hard questions about how they behave when things go wrong. That is what draws me to this space. I want to be part of building AI that holds up under scrutiny, not just under ideal conditions.

What gives me passion is the intersection of engineering and evaluation. I have spent time on both sides: building systems and stress testing them, writing code and then asking whether that code actually does what it claims to. That dual perspective feels important to me. Too often the people who build something are not the same people who interrogate it, and that gap is where failure hides. I want to close it.

For the broader engineering community, my vision is one where rigour and responsibility are not treated as separate concerns. The systems we design are not neutral. AI pipelines, data infrastructure, automated decisions: these things either serve people well or they do not, and engineers are closer to that outcome than anyone else. I think we have a genuine obligation to care about correctness the way we care about performance, and to build things that are worth trusting, not just things that work.

What I hope to carry forward is a practice shaped by both instincts: the technical drive to build things properly, and the critical eye to keep asking whether what I am building is actually good.

01 · Practice

Four habits that shaped how I build

Structure, attention, composition, and language. All four came from outside computer science and all four ended up inside it.

Music

Classical piano, then production, then code

I studied classical piano for years before I wrote a line of code, and later completed a music production certification through Ableton. Both taught the same lesson from opposite directions: a piece of music is a system with rules, and the interesting work starts once you understand the rules well enough to break them deliberately.

Reading a score is not far from reading a specification. You hold structure, timing, and intent in your head at once, and a small error propagates audibly and immediately. Production added the engineering half: signal chains, layering, and the discipline of iterating toward something rather than expecting it right on the first pass.

Eventually this became an actual project. Motif is a generative music engine I built in JavaScript on the Web Audio API where compositions are written as logic rather than as a fixed timeline. It exists because I wanted to know what happens when you treat a piece of music as a program instead of a recording.

Feeds into Systems thinking, iterative design, and one of the projects in Section 2.
Photography

Learning what to leave out of the frame

Photography is mostly a subtraction problem. The scene already contains everything, and the only real decision is what not to include. That skill transfers directly to two things I do constantly: deciding how data gets presented, and explaining technical work to people who do not need the implementation detail.

It also built patience with observation. A photograph worth keeping usually requires waiting and looking rather than acting, which is a useful counterweight to the instinct to start solving before you have understood the problem.

Feeds into Communicating upward, interface and dashboard decisions, knowing which detail to cut.
Drawing & visual design

Abstract drawing, and a bias toward how things look

I draw abstractly, which means working without a reference and making structural decisions as I go. It has made me unusually willing to throw away work that is technically fine but compositionally wrong, and that is a habit I now apply to code and to documents without much hesitation.

The practical result is that I care about the visual layer of technical work more than most engineers do. This portfolio and my developer site were designed as much as they were built, because I do not accept that presentation is separate from the work. If nobody can read it, the correctness of the thing underneath is theoretical.

Feeds into Interface and document design, willingness to discard and restart, this site.
Reading

Literature, and taking writing seriously

Reading fiction is where I learned that precision in language is not decoration. The distance between a sentence that lands and one that almost lands is usually a single word, and noticing that distance is a trainable skill rather than a gift.

This is the least obviously technical thing on the page and possibly the most useful.
